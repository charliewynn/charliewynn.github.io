I am the entire audience for a ten-show podcast network. I am also its commissioner, its editor-in-chief, and its harshest critic. I have never recorded a minute of audio for it, never edited a waveform, never uploaded a file. Every episode — researched, scripted, voiced, and delivered to my Spotify on a weekly schedule — is made by my AI assistant, to briefs I set, on subjects I want to understand.

As of this writing: ten shows (which turns out to be the cap on Spotify's save-for-later shows — we hit the ceiling and had to consolidate), more than forty episodes in the first month, and a release calendar that runs itself while I do my actual job. This is the most quietly life-changing of my AI projects, and it isn't close.

<!--more-->

## Why podcasts at all

I absorb long material better by ear than on the page. Always have, increasingly so. But the things I want to learn — tax law, how modern AI actually works, philosophy, what's on the Supreme Court's docket — do not come in podcast form at the depth I want, paced the way I want, starting from what I already know.

A generic show starts from the average listener. I don't need the average listener's starting point. My tax show can assume my actual financial life in outline. My AI show starts from a specific place: a computer science AI course I took in 2012 — A* search, hill climbing, the classical toolkit — and fills the gap forward to LLMs and agents, because that gap is exactly mine. No publisher would make that show. It has an audience of one and it is *perfect* for him.

So the network is a knowledge store, not entertainment that happens to be educational. That distinction produced the network's first law:

**Teach, don't mirror.** An episode has to carry the substance itself — the terms, the frameworks, the examples, the do's and don'ts — not reflect my own ideas back at me in a professional voice. I caught early episodes doing the flattering version and killed it. If I already know everything in an episode, the episode failed, however good it sounds.

## The lineup

Each series is its own show on Spotify, private to me, one episode a week on its own day:

- **Five and Five** — five stories from the week's news, plus a Vatican segment (I'm Catholic; the Pope is my beat). The flagship, and the exception to every interactive rule: it runs on a standing brief, no input from me at all. I just listen.
- **Taxes, Slowly** — exactly what it sounds like. One tax topic per episode, taken slowly, at the depth my actual returns require.
- **AI How It Works** — the 2012-to-now gap-filler described above. Episode four landed today.
- **Philosophy** — an idea per week, argued properly. Most recent: *what makes an argument good?*
- **The Docket** — what's moving through the courts, explained like it matters, because it does.
- **A developmental show** following how my three-year-old's mind is changing, week by week — pretend play becoming a two-person game, the arrival of "but why?" That one I won't name here. It's hers.
- **Story Workshop** — fiction craft. Currently snoozed for two months, by my choice; the system supports putting a series to sleep and waking it on a date, and the schedule just... holds.
- **Distilled Books** — whole novels distilled into episodes by a dedicated persona whose entire craft is this: structure, themes, and pronunciation. Twenty-one episodes across three series so far. The pronunciation part is not a joke — fantasy names are a minefield, the first Oathbringer episode had to be redone with corrected pronunciations, and that fix is now part of the craft.
- **Burn the Script** — a friend's published book, produced chapter by chapter as an audiobook. Chapter one is live; the rest follows as she releases chapters to me.

## How an episode gets made

I set a series up once: subject, starting point, depth, cadence, what "good" means for it. After that the pipeline is boring, which is the point. On the series' day, the episode is researched, scripted to the brief, voiced, and saved to that show on my Spotify. I get a short note that it's ready. I listen on a drive or a walk.

My job after that is the best job in media: **be the critic.** Steering used to work by pre-episode comprehension questions — the show would quiz me two days out and shape itself around my answers. I reversed that in November. It was homework, and it front-loaded my attention onto a show I hadn't heard yet. Now episodes are produced on their planned direction, and my *post-listen* feedback is the steering: questions I ask afterward, "go back to that part," "that was too shallow," "more like that one." The next episode is where it lands. It feels less like being managed and more like having a producer who actually listens to notes.

## What it costs and what it's worth

The honest accounting: a real chunk of setup taste. The teach-don't-mirror law, the steering reversal, the pronunciation craft, the show-cap consolidation — every one of those is a correction I made after hearing something that wasn't good enough. The network sounds like me because I spent a month being its picky audience of one, in writing.

What it's worth is harder to say without sounding like an ad, so I'll just describe a Tuesday: drive to work with a tax episode that assumes my life; walk at lunch with philosophers arguing about whether ideas exist; drive home with five news stories and the Pope. Every minute of it was made for exactly one listener's actual gaps. A month ago none of it existed. Now it's just... on, every week, like weather.

## The limits, because there are some

- **It can be confidently wrong in a nice voice.** A voiced error lands with more authority than a written one. My defence is the critic loop — I catch things, and corrections go into the next episode — but a show I know nothing about could teach me something false and I'd nod along. I treat the learning shows as a very good tutor, not a source of record.
- **The audience of one is also the bottleneck of one.** Ten shows is a lot of listening. Series snooze, backlogs form, and that's fine — but the network's appetite is set by my ears, not by its ability to produce.
- **It will never surprise me the way a great human host does.** It optimises toward my brief. The weird tangent, the guest I'd never have chosen — that serendipity is what real podcasts are for, and I still listen to those too.

But as a machine for turning "I wish I understood that" into a weekly show that starts exactly where I am — it's done, it works, and it's been running long enough that I've stopped being surprised when an episode is just... there, on schedule, good. That's the whole review, really. It's boring now. Boring is what finished looks like.
